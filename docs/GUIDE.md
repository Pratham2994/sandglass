# Sandglass guide

Generated by `npm run guide`. First how each system works and how to use it, then all 246 elements by group: what it is, what it does, and what to try.

## How the systems work

### Controls

- Left drag paints the selected element; right drag erases; the mouse wheel over the canvas changes the brush size; a middle click on the canvas picks up the element under the pointer.
- Heat, Cool and Wind (top of the library) are tools, not elements. The heat tool tops out at 1,500 °C; for more, use lava, thermite or lightning.
- Space pauses, the step button moves one frame, the slider slows time, and the scrubber rewinds up to 30 s (draw while rewound to start a new branch).
- Point at any cell to read it in the lens (bottom right): what it is, how hot, what it carries, current, pressure, light, and what it reacts with and why. Press I to pin the lens so you can scroll it.
- Press / to search; keys 1 to 9 pick recent elements; the list button opens the master list of every pair.
- The edges of the canvas hold liquids like a bowl, so "pour a pool on the floor" means just pour it along the bottom. Build a Wall bowl only when you want a small pool of its own. "Open sky" means nothing built above it.

### Heat and states

- Every cell has a real temperature. Things melt, boil, freeze and condense at their own points, and heat flows between touching cells.
- Red-hot things glow, and anything burning or glowing lights its surroundings.

### Fire

- Fire needs fuel, heat and air. Seal a fire in a box and it starves as its smoke replaces the air.
- Water, carbon dioxide, nitrogen and argon smother fire; oxygen, ozone, saltpeter and nitric acid feed it.
- Smoke fades to a faint haze, and smoke trapped under a roof clears after a while.

### Water, soil and keeping a garden wet

- Pour water on dirt and wait a few seconds: the dirt soaks it up and turns dark. Damp soil slowly passes water to drier soil beside it.
- Plants drink the soil dry in about a minute. To keep a garden alive, paint a thin cloud (one row) above it for gentle rain, or pour more water now and then. A cloud never throws lightning; a storm cloud does and can burn the garden.
- Do not flood a garden: a deep pool on top drowns small seedlings in shade. Point the lens at a leaf to see its water.

### Plants and light

- Seeds sprout on damp soil (dirt or sand with water). Plants grow only where light reaches them: open sky, through glass or water, or near a fire or lamp. A roof stops them.
- Acorns grow into trees with leaves; seeds into plants that flower at their tips; grass seed into grass; cactus seed on damp sand.
- Mushrooms are the opposite: they grow from spores on damp soil only in the dark. Dried plants turn brown and burn easily.

### Animals

- Paint animals where they live: fish into water, worms into soil, birds, bees and butterflies in the air, the rest on the ground. They head for food they can see within about 14 cells, eat it, and breed when fed.
- They die of heat, cold, or being out of their home too long (walkers drown in water, fish suffocate out of it).
- Food web: snakes eat mice and frogs, frogs and spiders eat insects, birds eat seeds and ants, fish eat seeds and algae, worms turn dead plants into dirt, termites chew wood.
- Bees and butterflies turn flowers into seeds. Butterflies lay eggs on leaves; the caterpillars eat leaves and grass, spin cocoons, and hatch as butterflies.
- Plague mist makes animals sick (a fever that spreads by touch and kills); sprinkle medicine on them to cure it.

### Chemistry

- Acids eat metals (giving off hydrogen), rock and wood; bases neutralise acids into warm water; carbonates and baking soda fizz out carbon dioxide.
- Salt, sugar and dyes dissolve into water, which then carries them (salt water conducts and freezes lower). Boiling the water leaves the crystals.
- Catalysts speed a reaction without being used up (manganese dioxide on hydrogen peroxide). Some reactions run away as they heat (glycerin on permanganate). Some need light (hydrogen and chlorine).
- Crystals grow from saturated solution; cabbage juice turns pink in acid and green in a base; yeast ferments sugar water into alcohol.

### Electricity

- Draw a closed loop of wire. Paint a battery onto the wire, then a bulb onto the wire elsewhere: current flows round the loop and the bulb glows. With no closed loop, nothing flows.
- A longer battery gives more volts, a thicker one more current; batteries in one loop help each other. Paint parts at least as wide as the wire, so no wire is left round them.
- Click a switch to open or close it. LEDs and diodes pass current one way; an LED needs a resistor or it burns out. A loop with nothing in it is a short circuit: the wire heats and the battery can burst into flame.
- Capacitors store charge, motors carry what rests on them, electromagnets pull iron while powered, solar panels power a loop in daylight, salt water conducts and splits into gas. The lens shows amps and volts.

### Pressure

- Liquid turning into gas in a sealed box (boiling water, a fizzing reaction) builds pressure until the walls burst. In the open it just drifts away.
- Build walls at least four cells thick to seal a box. A valve in the wall vents the pressure. Propane and air tanks rupture in heat or a blast.

### Structure

- Stone, brick, concrete, ice, glass and wood need holding up: each reaches only so far past a support (stone 6 cells, concrete 16, steel beam 60). Wall never falls.
- What nothing holds breaks off and falls as a block; a block sinks in liquid lighter than itself and floats on heavier. A long drop cracks stone into gravel.
- Rope and chain hold up what hangs below them; burn a rope and the load falls.

### Explosives

- TNT, gunpowder and fireworks go off from a flame; C4 needs a spark, a fuse or great heat. A long charge goes off as a fast wave from where it was lit.
- A fuse burns along like a rope and sets off what is at its end. Nitrogen triiodide goes off at the lightest touch.

### Nuclear

- A small piece of uranium only stays warm. Pack about 45 cells together and it goes critical: it heats fast, flings neutrons, melts down into corium and can blow apart.
- Control rods, lead, concrete and water soak up neutrons: push rods into a pile to calm it.

### Weather and sky

- Clouds rain (or snow below 0 °C); storm clouds rain, hail and throw lightning. Lightning melts sand into glass, lights fires and sends current down wires.
- Fireworks launch rockets that burst into falling embers; sparklers spit sparks as they burn.

## Elements

### Powders (25)

**Sand.** A fine, heavy powder.
Falls, piles into slopes and sinks through lighter liquids. Very hard to melt or eat away.
*Try:* Pour a heap and watch it slide into a slope. Strike it with a storm cloud, or melt it in lava: it turns into glass.

**Gravel.** Loose rock, a heavy powder.
Falls and piles. Forms where lava sets with nothing under it, and melts back into lava.
*Try:* Heat it past 1,000 °C (the heat tool or lava) and watch: it melts into lava.

**Ash.** A very light powder left by fire.
Drifts as it falls. Floats on water.
*Try:* Burn some wood to make it. Sprinkle it on water: it floats.

**Sawdust.** Fine wood dust, a very light powder.
Drifts and floats on water. Catches fire easily and burns fast.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Salt.** White crystals, a powder.
Falls and piles. Dissolves into water, making it heavier; boiling the water leaves the salt behind.
*Try:* Stir it into a pool of water: it disappears into the water. Boil the water away with the heat tool and the salt comes back.

**Sugar.** Sweet crystals, a powder.
Falls and piles. Dissolves into water even more than salt does, and burns when hot.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Dirt.** Earth, a heavy powder.
Falls and piles. Soaks up a lot of water and turns into dark, sticky mud.
*Try:* Pour water on it and wait: it darkens as it soaks. Drop a seed on damp dirt and a plant grows.

**Snow.** Loose frozen flakes, a very light powder.
Drifts down and settles in soft piles. Melts into water as it warms.
*Try:* Heat it past 0 °C (the heat tool) and watch: it melts into water.

**Shards.** Broken glass, a sharp powder.
Falls and piles in glittering heaps.
*Try:* Blow up some glass with TNT to get them; they pile like glittering sand.

**Baking soda.** A mild white powder and a base.
Falls and piles. Acid makes it fizz into carbon dioxide, and it dissolves a little in water.
*Try:* Make a heap and pour vinegar or lemon juice on it: it fizzes up bubbles.

**Graphite.** Soft grey carbon, a powder.
Falls and piles, and carries current where its grains touch, with some resistance, so a pile warms under load. In great heat it burns slowly into carbon dioxide.
*Try:* Pour it between two wire ends in a powered loop: it carries the current and warms up.

**Chalk.** Soft white carbonate, a powder.
Falls and piles. Acid fizzes it away into carbon dioxide.
*Try:* Pour vinegar or acid on it: it fizzes away into bubbles.

**Pumice.** Light, bubbly volcanic rock, a powder.
Falls and piles, yet floats on water. It soaks up water into its pores.
*Try:* Drop it into a pool of water: this rock floats.

**Saltpeter.** White crystals that carry oxygen, a powder.
Falls and piles, and dissolves in water. Fuel touching it burns fiercely.
*Try:* Mix it grain by grain with charcoal and light it: it keeps burning even inside a sealed box.

**Hail.** Hard balls of ice, a heavy powder.
Falls fast and bounces into piles. Melts into water as it warms.
*Try:* Heat it past 0 °C (the heat tool) and watch: it melts into water.

**Iodine.** Dark violet crystals, a heavy powder.
Falls and piles. Gently heated it turns straight into a violet vapour, which settles back into crystals as it cools.
*Try:* Heat it past 114 °C (the heat tool) and watch: it sublimes into iodine vapour.

**Manganese dioxide.** A black powder and a catalyst.
Falls and piles. Hydrogen peroxide touching it tears apart into water and a rush of oxygen, and the powder itself is never used up.
*Try:* Pour hydrogen peroxide and a little soap onto a heap of it: foam erupts upwards (elephant toothpaste).

**Copper sulfate.** Bright blue crystals, a powder.
Falls and piles, and dissolves into water, turning it blue. Blue crystals grow from that water onto a copper sulfate crystal.
*Try:* Stir a lot of it into a pool of water (the water turns blue), then drop in a blue crystal: crystals grow.

**Sodium acetate.** White needle crystals of sodium acetate.
Stays put. Warmed past 58 °C they melt back into hot ice, which stays liquid as it cools.
*Try:* Drop a single cell into a pool of hot ice: the pool turns to crystal. Heat crystals past 58 °C to make hot ice again.

**Potassium permanganate.** Dark purple crystals that carry oxygen, a powder.
Falls and piles, and dissolves into water, turning it purple. Glycerin touching it heats up by itself until it bursts into flame.
*Try:* Make a small heap and pour glycerin on it, then wait a few seconds: it bursts into flame by itself.

**Red dye.** Bright red dye powder.
Falls and piles, and dissolves into water, tinting it red as far as it spreads.
*Try:* Pour a pool of water on the floor and drop a pinch of red dye at one end: the colour spreads slowly through the water.

**Blue dye.** Deep blue dye powder.
Falls and piles, and dissolves into water, tinting it blue as far as it spreads.
*Try:* Drop red dye at one end of a pool of water and blue dye at the other: watch the two colours spread.

**Silver chloride.** A white light-sensitive powder, the stuff of old photographs.
Falls and piles. In strong light it slowly darkens into grey-black silver, so anything shading it leaves a pale print.
*Try:* Pour a thin layer under open sky and lay a wall shape over part of it: after a while the open part turns black, the covered part stays white.

**Exposed silver.** Silver chloride darkened by light: fine grains of silver, grey-black.
Falls and piles. There is no way back.

**Ammonium nitrate.** White fertiliser crystals, a powder.
Dissolving in water, it makes the water ice cold, as in a cold pack. It feeds fire, and great heat makes it explode.
*Try:* Stir it into a pool of water and point at the water: it gets ice cold. Heat it hard and it explodes.

### Liquids (24)

**Water.** A runny liquid.
Flows and settles flat. Boils into steam when hot, freezes into ice when cold, and cools what it touches.
*Try:* Pour it onto the floor: it spreads out flat from edge to edge. Pour it on fire and the fire goes out; pour it on lava and the lava turns black (obsidian).

**Oil.** A thick, slow liquid.
Floats on water. Burns into smoke once hot enough.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Lava.** Molten rock, extremely hot.
Flows slowly and glows. Sets fuel alight, boils water, and cools into stone (or gravel where nothing holds it up).
*Try:* Pour it into water: it hisses into steam and sets into black obsidian. Pour it next to wood: the wood catches fire.

**Acid.** A corrosive liquid.
Flows like water. Eats metal, wood and soft rock and is used up as it does; metal gives off hydrogen.
*Try:* Pour it onto an iron block: the iron fizzes away and bubbles of hydrogen rise. It eats stone and wood too, but not glass or gold.

**Alcohol.** A thin, light liquid.
Floats on water. Catches fire easily and burns clean into steam.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Vinegar.** A weak acid, mostly water.
Flows like water. Too weak to eat metal, but it fizzes with baking soda and neutralises bases.
*Try:* Pour it onto a heap of baking soda: it fizzes and bubbles.

**Bleach.** A strong cleaning base.
Flows like water. Mixed with any acid it gives off poisonous chlorine gas.
*Try:* Pour a little vinegar into a pool of bleach: a poisonous green gas (chlorine) rises. Keep plants away.

**Lye.** A caustic alkaline liquid.
Flows like water. It neutralises acid into water, warming as it does.
*Try:* Pour it into a pool of acid: the two cancel out into warm water.

**Liquid nitrogen.** Nitrogen cooled to a liquid, bitterly cold.
Flows and boils away fast into nitrogen gas, freezing whatever it touches.
*Try:* Pour it into a pool of water: the water freezes into ice while the nitrogen boils away.

**Liquid oxygen.** Oxygen cooled to a pale blue liquid, bitterly cold.
Flows and boils away into oxygen. A fuel it soaks burns with ferocious heat.
*Try:* Heat it past -183 °C (the heat tool) and watch: it boils into oxygen.

**Molten glass.** Melted sand, glowing and thick.
Oozes slowly. As it cools it sets into clear glass.
*Try:* Leave it to cool below 1,400 °C: it freezes into glass.

**Molten plastic.** Melted plastic, a hot sticky goo.
Oozes slowly and floats on water. Sets back into plastic as it cools, and burns if it gets hotter.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Hydrogen peroxide.** A pale blue liquid that carries spare oxygen.
Flows and sinks through water. It feeds fire, and breaks down into water and oxygen when heated or on rust.
*Try:* Pour it onto rust or onto manganese dioxide: it bubbles up oxygen.

**Acetone.** A sharp-smelling solvent.
Flows thin and floats on water. It dissolves plastic, and its vapour catches from the smallest flame.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire (or the heat tool, as it is hard to light): it catches and burns.

**Bromine.** A dark red, poisonous liquid, very heavy.
Flows and sinks through water. Warmed, it boils into a choking red vapour, and it kills plants.
*Try:* Heat it past 59 °C (the heat tool) and watch: it boils into bromine vapour.

**Soap.** Liquid soap.
Flows like water and floats on it. Gas bubbling through it (oxygen, carbon dioxide, hydrogen) whips it into foam.
*Try:* Pour hydrogen peroxide and soap onto manganese dioxide, or vinegar and soap onto baking soda: bubbly foam piles up.

**Foam.** A pile of soap bubbles, very light.
Heaps up, floats on everything and slowly pops away.

**Cabbage juice.** Red cabbage juice, a purple pH indicator.
Flows like water. Touching an acid (vinegar, acid) it turns pink; touching a base (lye, baking soda, ammonia) it turns green.
*Try:* Pour a long pool of it, add a drop of vinegar at one end and lye at the other: one end turns pink, the other green.

**Pink juice.** Cabbage juice turned pink by an acid.
Flows like water; a base turns it green.

**Green juice.** Cabbage juice turned green by a base.
Flows like water; an acid turns it pink.

**Ferrofluid.** Oil thick with tiny iron grains, a black liquid.
Flows, and a magnet or a powered electromagnet pulls it in, so it climbs towards them.
*Try:* Pour a pool of it and build a magnet just above it: the black liquid climbs towards the magnet.

**Hot ice.** A supercooled sodium acetate solution, a clear liquid.
Flows and stays liquid when cold, until it touches a sodium acetate crystal: then it sets into crystal in a spreading wave, giving off heat like a hand warmer.
*Try:* Pour a pool of hot ice, then drop one cell of sodium acetate into it: white crystal spreads through the whole pool and it warms up.

**Sulfuric acid.** A heavy, oily, very strong acid.
Flows and sinks through water, heating it fiercely as it mixes. It eats metal and rock, and tears the water out of sugar, leaving a black column of carbon.
*Try:* Make a heap of sugar and pour sulfuric acid on it: a black column of carbon grows up out of it with puffs of steam.

**Nitric acid.** A fuming, very strong acid that carries oxygen.
Flows like water. It eats metal and rock; copper it dissolves in a cloud of brown, poisonous nitrogen dioxide. Fuel soaked in it burns fiercely.
*Try:* Pour a little onto a strip of copper wire: the wire dissolves and brown gas rises.

### Gases (23)

**Steam.** Hot water vapour.
Rises and spreads. Turns back into water as it cools, so it rains.
*Try:* Leave it to cool below 100 °C: it condenses into water.

**Smoke.** What burning leaves behind, a warm gas.
Rises and fades to a faint haze as it ages. It is spent air that feeds no fire, so it smothers flames in a closed space.
*Try:* Burn some wood under a roof: the smoke gathers under it as a haze, then fades away.

**Hydrogen.** The lightest gas.
Shoots upwards. Burns in a flash into steam when hot and touching air or oxygen.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire (or the heat tool, as it is hard to light): it catches and burns.

**Oxygen.** A gas slightly heavier than air.
Sinks and pools low. Makes fire burn hotter and use its fuel faster.
*Try:* Cool it with the cool tool or liquid nitrogen below -183 °C: it condenses into liquid oxygen.

**Methane.** Natural gas, lighter than air.
Rises. Burns hot into steam when it meets heat and air.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire (or the heat tool, as it is hard to light): it catches and burns.

**Nitrogen.** The inert gas that makes up most of air.
Drifts slowly upwards. It feeds no fire, so it smothers flames it surrounds.
*Try:* Cool it with the cool tool or liquid nitrogen below -196 °C: it condenses into liquid nitrogen.

**Carbon dioxide.** A heavy, colourless gas.
Sinks and pools low like water. It feeds no fire, so it smothers flames; cooled hard it freezes into dry ice.
*Try:* Cool it with the cool tool or liquid nitrogen below -78 °C: it condenses into dry ice.

**Helium.** A very light, inert gas.
Rises fast and gathers at the top. It never burns or reacts.
*Try:* Release some under a roof: it shoots up and gathers against the ceiling.

**Propane.** A fuel gas, heavier than air.
Sinks and pools in low places. Its vapour catches from any flame and burns hot into steam.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire (or the heat tool, as it is hard to light): it catches and burns.

**Chlorine.** A poisonous yellow-green gas, very heavy.
Sinks and pools low. It kills plants and turns water it touches into acid.
*Try:* Let it drift onto plants: they wither. Let it touch water: the water turns into acid.

**Sulfur dioxide.** A choking gas, much heavier than air.
Sinks and pools low. It turns water it touches into acid and kills plants.
*Try:* Burn some sulfur under a cloud: the gas sinks onto water and turns it into acid rain.

**Cloud.** A soft white cloud of droplets, a gas.
Drifts and spreads. It rains gently, or snows when below freezing, until it runs dry; it never throws lightning.
*Try:* Paint one thin row of cloud above a garden: it rains gently and keeps the soil damp. When it is below 0 °C it snows.

**Storm cloud.** A dark, heavy thundercloud.
Drifts and spreads. It rains, or hails when below freezing, and now and then lets go a bolt of lightning that sets things alight.
*Try:* Paint it above a tree, a wire or a heap of sand and wait a few seconds: lightning strikes.

**Neon.** A rare inert gas, a little lighter than air.
Rises slowly. Current through it makes it glow orange-red, as in a neon sign; seal it in glass with wire at each end.
*Try:* Draw a glass tube, fill it with neon, put wire at both ends and connect them to a long battery: the tube glows orange.

**Ammonia.** A sharp, choking gas and a base, lighter than air.
Rises and spreads. It neutralises acid it touches into water, and kills plants.
*Try:* Pour it into a pool of acid: the two cancel out into warm water.

**Ozone.** A pale blue gas of energised oxygen, heavier than air.
Sinks slowly. It feeds fire even more fiercely than oxygen and poisons living things.
*Try:* Release it near a small fire: the fire roars. It also harms plants and animals.

**Acetylene.** A fuel gas, about as heavy as air.
Drifts and spreads. It catches from any flame and burns hotter than any other fuel, as in a cutting torch.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Iodine vapour.** A heavy violet vapour.
Sinks and pools low. As it cools it settles back into iodine crystals.
*Try:* Leave it to cool below 114 °C: it condenses into iodine.

**Argon.** A heavy inert gas.
Sinks and pools low. It never reacts, and it smothers flames by shutting out the air.
*Try:* Pour it over a small fire on the floor: the heavy gas smothers the flames.

**Bromine vapour.** A choking red-brown vapour, very heavy.
Sinks and pools low, kills plants, and condenses back into bromine as it cools.
*Try:* Leave it to cool below 59 °C: it condenses into bromine.

**Fog.** Cold white mist, as when dry ice meets warm water.
Heavy and cold, it sinks and creeps along the ground, then thins away.

**Hydrogen chloride.** A sharp, choking gas, heavier than air.
Sinks and pools low. It turns water it touches into acid. It forms with a flash where hydrogen and chlorine meet in light.

**Nitrogen dioxide.** A choking red-brown gas, heavier than air.
Sinks and pools low. It poisons living things, and turns water it touches into nitric acid.

### Solids (13)

**Wall.** An indestructible solid.
Never moves. Nothing melts, burns or eats it; use it for containers and floors.
*Try:* Draw floors, bowls and boxes with it. Nothing melts, burns or breaks it, so it holds anything.

**Ice.** Frozen water, a cold solid.
Stays put. Chills what it touches and melts back into water as it warms.
*Try:* Heat it past 0 °C (the heat tool) and watch: it melts into water.

**Stone.** Solid rock.
Stays put. Glows as it heats and melts back into lava.
*Try:* Heat it past 1,000 °C (the heat tool or lava) and watch: it melts into lava.

**Wood.** A solid fuel.
Stays put. Catches fire where it touches air once hot enough, and burns down to a little ash.
*Try:* Build a block, light one corner with fire, and watch the flames spread. Termites eat it into sawdust.

**Glass.** A clear, brittle solid.
Stays put and lets you see through it. Shatters into shards when a blast hits it.
*Try:* Heat it past 1,400 °C (the heat tool or lava) and watch: it melts into molten glass.

**Dry ice.** Frozen carbon dioxide, very cold.
Stays put and turns straight into gas without melting, chilling everything nearby.
*Try:* Drop a lump into a pool of warm water: it sinks and makes a thick white fog that creeps across the floor.

**Plastic.** A hard, bright synthetic.
Stays put and shrugs off acid. It melts at a modest heat into molten plastic and burns with thick smoke.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Obsidian.** Black volcanic glass, formed where water chills lava fast.
Stays put. A blast shatters it into sharp shards, and great heat melts it back into lava.
*Try:* Heat it past 1,000 °C (the heat tool or lava) and watch: it melts into lava.

**Diamond.** Pure crystal carbon, the hardest thing there is.
Stays put and moves heat superbly. In great heat it burns away into carbon dioxide.
*Try:* Heat it past 900 °C with the heat tool: it catches fire.

**Basalt.** Dark volcanic rock, very hard.
Stays put. Great heat melts it into lava.
*Try:* Heat it past 1,100 °C (the heat tool or lava) and watch: it melts into lava.

**Granite.** Speckled pink and grey rock, very hard.
Stays put. Only very great heat melts it into lava.
*Try:* Heat it past 1,250 °C (the heat tool or lava) and watch: it melts into lava.

**Sandstone.** Sand pressed into soft rock.
Stays put. A blast crumbles it back into sand.
*Try:* Set off TNT next to it: it crumbles back into sand.

**Blue crystal.** A clear blue crystal of copper sulfate.
Stays put, and grows into touching water that carries dissolved copper sulfate, taking the blue out of it.
*Try:* Drop it into blue copper sulfate water: it grows bigger, taking the blue out of the water.

### Building (20)

**Clay.** Fine, sticky earth, a heavy powder.
Falls and piles, and soaks up water. Fired in great heat, it bakes hard into brick.
*Try:* Make a lump and heat it with the heat tool (or set it in lava): it bakes into red brick.

**Brick.** Fired clay, a solid block.
Stays put and shrugs off heat. A strong blast breaks it into gravel.
*Try:* Build a box around a fire: the brick does not burn. Blow it up and it breaks into gravel.

**Cement.** Grey building powder.
Falls and piles. Water touching it is taken in, and it sets hard into concrete.
*Try:* Pour a heap, then pour a little water on it: it soaks up the water and sets hard into concrete.

**Concrete.** Set cement, a hard grey solid.
Stays put. Acid eats it slowly, and a big blast cracks it into gravel.
*Try:* Build long ledges with it: it holds out far without falling. Big blasts crack it into gravel.

**Limestone.** Pale sedimentary rock.
Stays put. Acid fizzes it away into carbon dioxide, and great heat bakes it into quicklime.
*Try:* Pour acid on it: it fizzes away into bubbles of carbon dioxide. Heat it hard and it turns into quicklime.

**Quicklime.** Burnt lime, a white caustic powder and a strong base.
Falls and piles. Water makes it seethe into slaked lime, hot enough to boil.
*Try:* Drop a little water on it and point at it: it gets hot enough to boil the water.

**Slaked lime.** A soft white powder and a base.
Falls and piles. It neutralises acids into water.
*Try:* Pour it into a pool of acid: the two cancel out into warm water.

**Marble.** Polished white rock, a carbonate.
Stays put. Acid fizzes it away into carbon dioxide, and great heat bakes it into quicklime.
*Try:* Pour acid on it: it fizzes away.

**Epoxy resin.** Part one of a two-part glue: a clear syrup.
Oozes slowly. On its own it stays runny; touching hardener, both set together into hard epoxy, getting warm.
*Try:* Pour resin and hardener side by side: where they meet they set into hard amber epoxy.

**Epoxy hardener.** Part two of a two-part glue: an amber syrup.
Oozes slowly. Touching resin, both set together into hard epoxy.

**Epoxy.** Set two-part epoxy, a tough clear amber solid.
Stays put and reaches far past its support. It burns only in strong heat.
*Try:* Heat it past 450 °C with the heat tool: it catches fire.

**UV resin.** A clear liquid resin.
Flows slowly, and stays runny in the dark; strong light cures it from the top down into a hard clear solid.
*Try:* Pour a pool of it on the floor under open sky: it slowly sets hard from the top. Under a roof it stays runny.

**Cured resin.** UV resin cured hard by light, a clear glassy solid.
Stays put and reaches well past its support. It burns in strong heat.
*Try:* Heat it past 420 °C with the heat tool: it catches fire.

**Superglue.** A thin, clear glue.
Flows freely, and sets hard in a moment on its own; touching water it sets at once.
*Try:* Draw a line of it, then splash water across it: it sets hard at once where the water touches.

**Glow paint.** Paint full of glow-in-the-dark crystals.
Stays put. It soaks up light while lit, then shines soft green in the dark for a while, fading as its stored light runs out.
*Try:* Paint some in the open for a few seconds, then cover it with a roof or dirt: it keeps glowing green for a while.

**Steel beam.** A rolled steel girder.
Stays put and holds itself out a very long way from its support, so it can bridge wide gaps that stone or brick never could. Great heat softens it into molten iron.
*Try:* Build two brick towers apart, then lay a steel beam across the gap: it holds. A stone bridge that long would break and fall.

**Rope.** Twisted hemp rope.
Hangs from whatever it is tied to and holds up what hangs below it, but it pushes nothing up and holds nothing sideways. It burns, and whatever it held falls.
*Try:* Hang a rope down from a wall ceiling and put a stone block at its end: it hangs. Set the rope on fire and the block drops.

**Chain.** Iron chain.
Hangs from whatever it is fixed to and holds up what hangs below it, but nothing sideways. It will not burn, though great heat melts it.
*Try:* Hang a block from a wall ceiling on a chain: fire does not drop it, but lava melts the chain.

**Glue.** A thick white glue.
Oozes slowly, then after a few seconds it sets hard into dried glue, sticking in place.
*Try:* Pour it where you want things to stick: it sets hard in a few seconds.

**Dried glue.** Glue that has set into a clear, hard solid.
Stays put. It burns in strong heat.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

### Metals (29)

**Iron.** A dense metal.
Stays put and moves heat quickly. Acid eats it, giving off hydrogen.
*Try:* Heat it past 1,538 °C (thermite or lightning) and watch: it melts into molten iron.

**Mercury.** A liquid metal, very heavy.
Flows and sinks through almost everything. Carries charge like a wire.
*Try:* Make a little trough of wall between two wire ends and pour mercury into it: it carries current like wire. Most things float on it.

**Molten copper.** Melted wire, bright and very hot.
Flows and sinks through lighter liquids. Carries charge, sets fuel alight, and sets back into wire as it cools.
*Try:* Leave it to cool below 1,085 °C: it freezes into wire.

**Gold.** A soft, very heavy yellow metal.
Stays put. Acid cannot touch it; it carries charge and heat superbly, and melts into molten gold.
*Try:* Heat it past 1,064 °C (the heat tool or lava) and watch: it melts into molten gold.

**Molten gold.** Melted gold, glowing and very heavy.
Flows and sinks through almost everything. Sets back into gold as it cools.
*Try:* Leave it to cool below 1,064 °C: it freezes into gold.

**Aluminium.** A light silvery metal.
Stays put. Acid eats it, giving off hydrogen, and it melts at a modest heat into molten aluminium.
*Try:* Heat it past 660 °C (the heat tool or lava) and watch: it melts into molten aluminium.

**Molten aluminium.** Melted aluminium, dull red and runny.
Flows and sinks through water. Sets back into aluminium as it cools.
*Try:* Leave it to cool below 660 °C: it freezes into aluminium.

**Lead.** A dull, very heavy grey metal.
Stays put. It melts easily into molten lead, and acid eats it slowly.
*Try:* Heat it past 327 °C (the heat tool or lava) and watch: it melts into molten lead.

**Molten lead.** Melted lead, hot and very heavy.
Flows and sinks through almost everything. Sets back into lead as it cools.
*Try:* Leave it to cool below 327 °C: it freezes into lead.

**Rust.** Flaky red iron oxide, a powder.
Falls and piles. It forms where water or oxygen slowly eats at iron.
*Try:* Leave iron sitting in water for a while and it turns red-brown. Pour hydrogen peroxide on rust: it bubbles out oxygen.

**Sodium.** A soft, light silvery metal.
Stays put. Water touching it makes it fizz violently into lye and burning hydrogen.
*Try:* Drop a small piece into a pool of water: it fizzes, pops and sets the gas alight.

**Magnesium.** A light silvery metal.
Stays put. Once very hot it burns with a blinding white flame, hot enough to set off thermite.
*Try:* Heat a strip with the heat tool until it catches: it burns blinding white. Touch the burning strip to thermite to light it.

**Potassium.** A very soft, light metal.
Stays put. Water touching it makes it burst into lye and burning hydrogen, even more fiercely than sodium.
*Try:* Drop a tiny piece into water from a distance: it bursts into flame on the surface.

**Molten iron.** Melted iron, white-hot and heavy.
Flows and sinks through water and rock. Sets back into iron as it cools.
*Try:* Leave it to cool below 1,538 °C: it freezes into iron.

**Silver.** A bright, heavy white metal.
Stays put. It carries charge and heat better than any other metal, and melts into molten silver.
*Try:* Heat it past 962 °C (the heat tool or lava) and watch: it melts into molten silver.

**Molten silver.** Melted silver, glowing and heavy.
Flows and sinks through almost everything. Sets back into silver as it cools.
*Try:* Leave it to cool below 962 °C: it freezes into silver.

**Tin.** A soft silvery metal.
Stays put. It melts at a low heat into molten tin, and acid eats it, giving off hydrogen.
*Try:* Heat it past 232 °C (the heat tool or lava) and watch: it melts into molten tin.

**Molten tin.** Melted tin, bright and runny.
Flows and sinks through water. Sets back into tin as it cools.
*Try:* Leave it to cool below 232 °C: it freezes into tin.

**Steel.** Hard iron alloy.
Stays put. Acid eats it slowly and water rusts it; in great heat it melts into molten iron.
*Try:* Heat it past 1,500 °C (thermite or lightning) and watch: it melts into molten iron.

**Zinc.** A bluish-grey metal.
Stays put and carries charge. Acid fizzes it away, giving off hydrogen.
*Try:* Pour acid on it: it fizzes away and gives off hydrogen bubbles.

**Uranium.** A dense, radioactive metal.
Stays put and keeps itself warm from its own decay, and poisons living things it touches. Packed together in a big enough pile it goes critical: a chain reaction heats it until it melts down, and can blow it apart.
*Try:* Paint a block about 14 cells wide and point at it: it heats up fast and can melt down. Push control rods into it to calm it.

**Lithium.** The lightest metal, soft and silvery.
Stays put. Water touching it fizzes steadily into lye and hydrogen, more gently than sodium.
*Try:* Drop it into water: it fizzes gently and gives off hydrogen.

**Titanium.** A light, very strong grey metal.
Stays put. Acid cannot eat it, and it barely conducts heat.
*Try:* Pour acid on it: nothing happens. It barely passes heat along.

**Platinum.** A very heavy, silvery noble metal.
Stays put and nothing eats it. Hydrogen touching it catches and burns into steam.
*Try:* Release hydrogen so it drifts onto platinum: the hydrogen bursts into flame.

**Gallium.** A soft silvery metal.
Stays put, but the warmth of a hand melts it into liquid gallium.
*Try:* Heat it past 30 °C (the heat tool) and watch: it melts into liquid gallium.

**Liquid gallium.** Melted gallium, a cool silvery liquid.
Flows and sinks through water. Sets back into gallium just below body warmth.
*Try:* Leave it to cool below 30 °C: it freezes into gallium.

**Corium.** Molten nuclear fuel from a meltdown, white-hot and very heavy.
Oozes slowly and sinks through almost everything. It keeps heating itself while enough of it is together, and sets back into uranium only if it cools.
*Try:* Make it by letting a big uranium pile melt down. It keeps glowing and creeping while enough of it is together.

**Steel wool.** A tangle of fine steel threads.
Stays put. So thin it heats fast: a battery across it makes it glow and catch, and it burns away in air into rust, faster still in oxygen.
*Try:* Draw a wire loop, paint a thick battery onto it and a small pad of steel wool onto it: the wool glows, catches fire and burns into rust.

**Iron filings.** Fine iron dust, a heavy powder.
Falls and piles, and a magnet pulls it in. It rusts in water, and acid eats it.
*Try:* Pour them near a magnet or a powered electromagnet: they jump towards it.

### Fuels (18)

**Coal.** Black lumps of fossil fuel, a heavy powder.
Falls and piles. Hard to light, but once going it burns hot for a very long time.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire (or the heat tool, as it is hard to light): it catches and burns.

**Charcoal.** Charred wood, a light black powder.
Falls and piles. It catches more easily than coal and glows hot for a long time.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Sulfur.** Yellow crystals, a powder.
Falls and piles. It burns with a blue flame into choking sulfur dioxide.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Petrol.** A thin, volatile fuel.
Flows freely and floats on water. Its vapour catches from the smallest flame, so a pool goes up at once.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Wax.** Soft, pale wax.
Stays put. It melts at a gentle heat into molten wax and burns slowly, like a candle.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Molten wax.** Melted wax, warm and runny.
Flows and floats on water. It sets back into wax as it cools, and burns once it gets hot.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Paper.** Thin sheets of wood pulp.
Stays put. It catches at the lightest flame and burns up quickly into ash.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Rubber.** Tough black rubber.
Stays put and never carries charge. Hard to light, but it burns long and hot.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Thermite.** A grey-red metal powder that carries its own oxygen.
Falls and piles. A flame will not light it, but great heat does, and it burns white-hot with no air, leaving iron.
*Try:* Make a heap and heat it with the heat tool or burning magnesium (a flame is not enough): it burns white-hot and leaves molten iron.

**Tar.** Thick black pitch.
Creeps along very slowly and sinks through water. Hard to light, but it burns for a long time.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Napalm.** A sticky fuel gel.
Oozes slowly and floats on water. Its vapour catches at once, and it burns fiercely for a long time, even on water.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Kerosene.** A clear lamp fuel.
Flows and floats on water. Once warm its vapour catches easily, and it burns steadily.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Cotton.** Soft white fibre, a very light powder.
Drifts as it falls and soaks up a lot of water. Dry, it catches fast and burns quickly.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Phosphorus.** Waxy white phosphorus.
Stays put. In barely warm air it bursts into flame by itself, so it is kept under water, where it is safe.
*Try:* Keep it under water. Drain the water away (erase it) and it bursts into flame by itself.

**Calcium carbide.** Grey-black lumps, a heavy powder.
Falls and piles. Water makes it hiss out acetylene gas, leaving slaked lime.
*Try:* Pour a little water on it, then bring a flame close: the gas it gives off burns very hot.

**Peat.** Dark, fibrous bog soil, a powder.
Falls and piles and soaks up a lot of water. Dried out, it smoulders for a very long time.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Glycerin.** A thick, sweet, clear liquid.
Oozes slowly and sinks through water. It burns once very hot; mixed with potassium permanganate it warms by itself and, after a pause, bursts into flame.
*Try:* Make a small heap of potassium permanganate and pour glycerin on it, then wait: after a few seconds it smokes and bursts into flame.

**Fire ice.** Methane hydrate: ice with natural gas locked inside.
Stays put and very cold. Warmed past 5 °C it falls apart into water and methane, and a flame sets the lump burning like ice on fire.
*Try:* Paint a lump and touch it with fire as it starts to bubble: it burns, and the flames sit on top of ice.

### Energy (7)

**Fire.** Loose flames.
Rise and burn out quickly. Light anything flammable they touch; a lasting fire needs fuel and air.
*Try:* Touch it to wood, oil or paper: they catch and burn down to ash or smoke.

**Spark.** A flash of electric charge.
Jumps into any conductor it touches and runs along it; on its own it fades at once.
*Try:* Draw a wire line with TNT at the far end, then drop a spark on the near end: it races along and sets the TNT off.

**Lightning.** A bolt of lightning.
Strikes straight down at once in a jagged line. Where it lands it pours in heat, lights flames and sends current through anything that conducts.
*Try:* Paint it high above sand, a tree or a wire: it strikes straight down, melting sand to glass and setting things alight.

**Plasma.** Glowing, electrified air left by lightning.
Flickers out in a moment. It lights anything flammable it touches.
*Try:* Made by lightning: anything that burns catches fire where it touches.

**Rocket.** A lit firework climbing fast.
Rises in a straight line, trailing sparks, then bursts into embers.
*Try:* Light a heap of firework powder to launch these.

**Ember.** A bright burning fleck from a firework.
Drifts down, swaying, and burns out. It can still light what it lands on.
*Try:* Launch fireworks to make them; they float down and can start new fires where they land.

**Neutron.** A fast neutron, flung out when uranium splits.
Flies straight until it hits something; striking uranium, it adds to the chain reaction. Lead, concrete, water and control rods soak neutrons up.

### Explosives (11)

**TNT.** A packed explosive block.
Stays put. Heat or a flame sets it off: the whole block blasts outwards, throwing what is nearby.
*Try:* Build a block next to some stone, then touch it with fire: it blows up and throws what is near.

**Gunpowder.** A dark explosive powder.
Falls and piles. Lit, it goes off grain by grain in quick small bangs.
*Try:* Pour a long thin trail and light one end with fire: it fizzes along the trail like a fuse.

**Nitro.** An oily explosive liquid.
Flows slowly. Goes off when heated, lit, or dropped hard.
*Try:* Build a small block next to some stone and touch it with fire: it blows up.

**C4.** A putty-like plastic explosive.
Stays put. A flame alone will not set it off, but a spark or great heat does, with a huge blast.
*Try:* Build a block. A flame does nothing; run a fuse into its side and light the fuse, or touch it with a spark on a wire: a huge blast.

**Nuke.** A nuclear bomb, a heavy dark powder.
Falls and piles. A hard landing, a flame or a spark sets it off: a blinding flash, a fireball that vaporises everything near, a shockwave that throws and shatters what is further out, a rising mushroom cloud, then fallout drifting down.
*Try:* Paint a dab of it high up over a town of brick, wood and trees: it falls, lands, and goes off. If it is already on the floor, touch it with fire.

**Fallout.** Grey radioactive dust left by a nuke.
Drifts down slowly and settles. Any animal it touches falls sick, as with plague. It loses its harm and turns to ash after a while.
*Try:* Set off a nuke near some mice or birds: the dust that drifts down afterwards makes them sick. Sprinkle medicine to save them.

**Firework.** A packed firework powder.
Falls and piles. A flame or heat launches each grain as a rocket that bursts high up into falling coloured embers.
*Try:* Pour a small heap and touch it with fire: rockets shoot up and burst into colourful sparks.

**Smoke bomb.** A packed grey powder.
Falls and piles. Lit, it smoulders slowly at a low heat, pouring out thick smoke.
*Try:* Light a heap inside a box: thick smoke pours out and fills it.

**Fuse.** A slow-burning cord.
Stays put. Lit at one end, it burns steadily along its whole length like a rope, and sets off any explosive at the far end, even C4.
*Try:* Draw a line from a block of C4 or TNT, then touch the far end with fire: it burns slowly along and sets the charge off.

**Nitrogen triiodide.** Dark purple-black crystals, the touchiest explosive there is.
Stays put where it dried. Anything moving against it, even a falling grain of sand or a drop of water, sets it off with a sharp purple crack; so does the least warmth.
*Try:* Paint a little on a ledge, then drop one grain of sand onto it: it goes off with a sharp crack.

**Sparkler.** A stick of slow sparkler compound.
Stays put. Lit, it burns slowly along, spitting bright sparks into the air all the while.
*Try:* Draw a thin line and touch one end with fire: it burns slowly along, spitting bright sparks.

### Electronics (13)

**Wire.** Thin copper.
Stays put and carries current with almost no resistance. A normal load barely warms it, but a short circuit heats it fast: with two or more cells behind it, red-hot and then molten.
*Try:* Draw a closed loop of wire, then paint a battery onto it and a bulb onto it: the bulb lights up.

**Battery.** A power cell.
Stays put. Painted into a loop of wire, it drives current round the loop, whichever way it fits; with no closed loop, nothing flows. Each cell along the wire adds 1.5 volts, and a thicker battery gives more current. It runs flat with use, and a long short circuit can make it burst into flame.
*Try:* Draw a closed loop of wire, paint a battery onto the wire, and a bulb elsewhere on the loop: the bulb glows. A loop with no bulb gets hot and can catch fire.

**Bulb.** A light bulb filament.
Stays put and carries current, which heats it until it glows: one cell on one battery is white-hot, a longer filament or bulbs in series glow dimmer. It fades when the power stops.
*Try:* Paint a small bulb onto a wire loop that has a battery: it glows white-hot. A big bulb needs a longer battery.

**Switch.** A knife switch.
Stays put. Click it to open or close the circuit: closed (bright), current passes as through wire; open (dark red), nothing passes.
*Try:* Paint it onto the wire of a working circuit, then click it: the bulb goes dark. Click again to turn it back on.

**Pressure plate.** A sprung contact plate.
Stays put. It closes the circuit while anything heavier than a gas rests on top of it, so falling sand, water or an animal can switch a light on.
*Try:* Paint it onto the top of a wire loop with a battery and a bulb, then drop a little sand onto the plate: the bulb lights up.

**Thermostat.** A bimetal strip.
Stays put and lets current through while cool; above 60 °C it bends open, and below 45 °C it closes again, so it can hold a heater at a steady warmth.
*Try:* In a loop with a long battery, put a resistor and a thermostat touching each other: the resistor heats, the thermostat clicks off, it cools, and it clicks on again.

**Resistor.** Carbon film that resists current, turning it into heat.
Stays put. One cell keeps an LED from burning out; a long run makes a heating element that glows.
*Try:* Paint a small piece into a loop that has an LED: the LED lights without burning out. A big resistor on a long battery glows like a heater.

**Diode.** A one-way valve for current.
Stays put and lets current through only rightwards or upwards; with the current flowing the other way round the loop, everything stays dark.
*Try:* Paint it into a loop together with a second diode further round: the bulb stays dark, because the two face opposite ways round the loop.

**LED.** A red light-emitting diode.
Lets current through only rightwards or upwards and glows bright red without getting hot. More than 0.6 amps burns it out, so put a resistor in line.
*Try:* Paint an LED and a small resistor onto a wire loop with a battery: the LED glows red. Without the resistor it burns out at once.

**Capacitor.** Stores charge.
Stays put. It charges while current flows through it and gives the charge back when the power is gone, lighting a bulb for a moment. Overcharged past 12 volts, as lightning does, it pops.
*Try:* Paint it into a loop with a battery and a bulb, wait a few seconds, then erase the battery: the bulb stays lit for a moment.

**Motor.** An electric motor.
Stays put. While current runs through it, it turns and carries whatever rests on top of it along like a conveyor; reverse the current and it runs the other way.
*Try:* Paint a long flat strip of motor into a powered loop and drop sand on it: the sand rides along the strip.

**Electromagnet.** Copper wire coiled round an iron core.
Stays put. While current runs through it, it pulls iron filings like a magnet, harder the more current; cut the power and it lets them go.
*Try:* Paint it into a wire loop with a battery and pour iron filings nearby: they jump towards it. Erase the battery and they drop.

**Solar panel.** A photovoltaic cell.
Stays put and works like a battery that never runs flat, but its voltage follows the light on it: full in open daylight, weak in shade, nothing in the dark. A longer panel gives more volts.
*Try:* Paint it onto a wire loop with a small bulb, under open sky: the bulb glows. Build a roof over it and the bulb goes dark.

### Devices (10)

**Heater.** A hot plate.
Stays put and holds itself at a steady high heat, warming whatever touches it.
*Try:* Build a hot plate, then put ice, water or a block of metal on it: they warm up, melt or boil.

**Cooler.** A cold plate.
Stays put and holds itself far below freezing, chilling whatever touches it.
*Try:* Build a cold plate and pour water on it: the water freezes into ice.

**Clone.** A copying block.
Stays put. It learns the first element that touches it and keeps making more of it in the space around.
*Try:* Touch it with any element, for example a little water: it keeps making more of it around itself.

**Void.** A hole in the world.
Stays put and deletes anything that touches it, except walls and other devices.
*Try:* Build it at the bottom of a pit: anything that falls into it disappears.

**Fan.** A blower.
Stays put and pushes a steady stream of air upwards, lifting gases, flames and light powders.
*Try:* Build it under some smoke or sawdust: it blows them upwards.

**Magnet.** A strong permanent magnet.
Stays put and pulls iron filings towards it from a short distance.
*Try:* Pour iron filings a few cells away: they creep towards it and stick.

**Control rod.** A boron carbide rod that soaks up neutrons.
Stays put. Set among uranium, it slows or stops the chain reaction; pull it out and the pile heats up.
*Try:* Build a big uranium block, then push a column of control rod down through its middle: the block stops heating.

**Valve.** A pressure relief valve.
Stays put and seals like a wall until the air pressure on it climbs past its setting; then it opens (bright) and lets gas through, closing again as the pressure falls.
*Try:* Build a box with thick glass walls and a valve in the lid, put water inside on a heater: the valve opens and lets steam out.

**Propane tank.** A steel tank full of propane.
Stays put. Heated past 250 °C or hit by a strong blast, it ruptures: the propane bursts out in a cloud that a single flame turns into a fireball.
*Try:* Put a tank in a fire and stand back: it bursts and the gas explodes into a fireball.

**Air tank.** A cylinder of compressed air.
Stays put. Heated past 300 °C or hit by a blast, it bursts with a sharp shock of pressure and no flame, throwing what is nearby and shattering glass.
*Try:* Put a tank next to a sheet of glass and heat the tank: it bursts with a bang that shatters the glass.

### Food (20)

**Flour.** Fine white powder, very light.
Drifts as it falls. It catches easily and burns fast where air reaches it, but soaks up water into a paste that will not burn.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Honey.** Thick golden syrup.
Oozes very slowly and sinks through water. Sticky and sweet; it does not burn.
*Try:* Put a few bees near it: they eat it and breed. It oozes very slowly.

**Caramel.** Melted, browned sugar.
Oozes very slowly. It burns once it gets very hot.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Meat.** Raw red meat.
Stays put. Warm it and it cooks; left in water it slowly rots.
*Try:* Put it on a heater: it browns into cooked meat. Leave it in water and it slowly rots into dirt.

**Cooked meat.** Browned, cooked meat.
Stays put. Overdone it chars into charcoal, and in open air it burns.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Popcorn kernel.** Hard yellow kernels, a powder.
Falls and piles. Heat them and they pop into light, fluffy popcorn.
*Try:* Make a heap on a heater: the kernels pop into fluffy popcorn that jumps up.

**Popcorn.** Popped corn, a very light powder.
Drifts down softly and floats on water. It burns easily.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Chocolate.** A dark, sweet block.
Stays put. A little warmth melts it into molten chocolate.
*Try:* Heat it past 35 °C (the heat tool) and watch: it melts into molten chocolate.

**Molten chocolate.** Melted chocolate, a thick sweet sauce.
Oozes slowly and sinks through water. Sets back into chocolate as it cools.
*Try:* Leave it to cool below 35 °C: it freezes into chocolate.

**Egg.** A raw egg.
Stays put. Warm it and it cooks; left in water it slowly rots.
*Try:* Put it on a heater: it cooks. Snakes eat eggs.

**Cooked egg.** A cooked egg, white and yellow.
Stays put. Overdone it chars into charcoal; left in water it slowly rots.
*Try:* Keep heating it on a heater: it chars black.

**Soda.** Fizzy water full of dissolved carbon dioxide.
Flows like water. Warmed, it fizzes out its gas and goes flat.
*Try:* Drop mints into a pool of soda: foam shoots up. Warm it and it goes flat.

**Dough.** Flour mixed with water, a soft sticky lump.
Falls and slumps. Baked past 180 °C it rises into bread.
*Try:* Mix flour and water, then put the dough on a heater: it bakes into bread, then toast.

**Bread.** A golden loaf.
Stays put. Heated past 260 °C it toasts dark; mould loves it when damp, and mice eat it.
*Try:* Keep heating it on a heater: it turns into toast, then catches fire.

**Toast.** Bread browned by heat, crisp and dark.
Stays put. A little more heat and it catches fire.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Mints.** Chalky mint sweets, a powder.
Falls and piles. Dropped into soda, their rough surface tears the gas out of it at once: the soda erupts into a tower of foam.
*Try:* Pour a pool of soda on the floor, then drop a few mints in: foam shoots up and overflows.

**Milk.** Fresh milk, a white liquid.
Flows like water and sinks just below it. An acid (lemon juice, vinegar) curdles it into lumps of curds.
*Try:* Pour a pool of milk on the floor, then add a splash of lemon juice or vinegar: white lumps (curds) form.

**Curds.** Soft white lumps of curdled milk, a powder.
Falls and settles. Mice love them; left in water they rot.

**Lemon juice.** Sour lemon juice, a weak acid.
Flows like water. Too weak to eat metal, but it fizzes with baking soda, curdles milk and turns cabbage juice pink.
*Try:* Pour it onto baking soda: it fizzes. Pour it into milk: curds form. Pour it into cabbage juice: it turns pink.

**Yeast.** Living baker's yeast, a light powder.
Falls and piles. In sugary water it ferments the sugar into alcohol, bubbling out carbon dioxide.
*Try:* Stir sugar into a pool of water, then add yeast: bubbles rise, and the sugar water slowly turns into alcohol.

### Life (33)

**Seed.** A small seed, a light powder.
Falls, sinks in water and rests. On damp soil (wet dirt or sand), it sprouts into a plant, even under a puddle.
*Try:* Pour water on dirt and wait a few seconds, then drop a seed on top, under open sky: a plant grows and flowers in about 20 seconds.

**Plant.** A living green plant.
Its roots drink from damp soil or water and pass it up the stem; it grows while it has water and puts out leaves. Dried out, it withers. Burns, and acid eats it.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Dry plant.** A plant that ran out of water, dry and brown.
Stays put. Catches fire very easily and burns fast, leaving ash.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Grass seed.** Tiny pale seeds, a light powder.
Falls and sinks in water. On damp soil it sprouts into a short tuft of grass.
*Try:* Scatter it on damp dirt under open sky: a carpet of grass grows.

**Grass.** Short living blades.
Stays put and drinks from damp soil. Dried out it withers into dry plant, and it burns easily.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Acorn.** A heavy brown seed, a powder.
Falls and sinks in water. On damp soil it sprouts into a tall, branching tree.
*Try:* Drop it on damp dirt with plenty of space above, under open sky: a tree grows with a thick trunk and leaves.

**Tree.** A living woody trunk.
Grows tall and branches, drinking from damp soil and putting out leaves. Dead and dry, it is plain wood.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Leaves.** Living green leaves on a tree.
Stay put and take water from the branch. Dried out they wither, and they burn easily.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Flower.** A bright bloom at the tip of a plant.
Stays put and takes water from the stem. Dried out it withers, and it burns easily.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Fish.** A small fish.
Swims about in water, eating seeds that sink to it and breeding when fed. Out of water it suffocates; heat or cold kill it.
*Try:* Pour a pool of water on the floor and paint a few fish into it, then sprinkle seeds or algae on the water: they swim up, eat and breed.

**Ant.** A busy little ant.
Walks over the ground and climbs steps, eating anything sweet or meaty and breeding when fed. Water drowns it; heat or cold kill it.
*Try:* Paint a few onto the ground, close to some sugar or honey or caramel: they head for the food, eat it and breed.

**Worm.** An earthworm.
Digs through soil, turning dead plants, paper and ash into rich dirt, and breeds as it eats. Dried out above ground, it dies.
*Try:* Paint a few into damp dirt, close to some dry plant or paper or ash: they head for the food, eat it and breed.

**Bird.** A small bird.
Flies about in the open air, eating seeds and ants and breeding when fed. Water drowns it; heat or cold kill it.
*Try:* Paint a few into open air, close to some seed or grass seed or acorn: they head for the food, eat it and breed.

**Bee.** A buzzing bee.
Flies about, visiting flowers and turning them into seeds, and breeds when it finds honey. Water drowns it; heat or cold kill it.
*Try:* Grow some flowers (seeds on damp dirt in daylight), then release a few bees nearby: they fly to the flowers and turn them into seeds.

**Spider.** A hunting spider.
Walks over the ground and climbs steps, catching ants, bees and termites, and breeds when fed. Water drowns it; heat or cold kill it.
*Try:* Paint a few onto the ground, close to some ant or bee or termite: they head for the food, eat it and breed.

**Termite.** A pale wood-eating insect.
Walks over the ground, chewing wood and trees into sawdust and eating the sawdust, breeding as it eats. Water drowns it.
*Try:* Paint a few onto the ground, close to some wood or tree or sawdust: they head for the food, eat it and breed.

**Snail.** A slow garden snail.
Creeps over the ground, grazing plants, leaves, grass and flowers, and breeds as it eats. Water drowns it; heat or cold kill it.
*Try:* Paint a few onto the ground, close to some plant or leaves or grass: they head for the food, eat it and breed.

**Frog.** A small green frog.
Hops over the ground catching ants, bees, spiders, termites and snails, and breeds when fed. Water drowns it; heat or cold kill it.
*Try:* Paint a few onto the ground, close to some ant or bee or spider: they head for the food, eat it and breed.

**Mouse.** A quick little mouse.
Runs over the ground eating seeds, flour, popcorn, chocolate and cooked meat, and breeds when fed. Water drowns it; heat or cold kill it.
*Try:* Paint a few onto the ground, close to some seed or grass seed or acorn: they head for the food, eat it and breed.

**Snake.** A hunting snake.
Slides over the ground catching mice and frogs, and breeds when fed. Water drowns it; heat or cold kill it.
*Try:* Paint a few onto the ground, close to some mouse or frog or egg: they head for the food, eat it and breed.

**Cactus seed.** Tiny dark seeds, a powder.
Falls and sinks in water. On damp soil or sand it sprouts into a cactus.
*Try:* Wet some sand with water, then drop the seed on it in daylight: a cactus grows, and it survives long dry spells.

**Cactus.** A fat, spiny desert plant.
Grows slowly and stores water, losing it so slowly that it outlasts a drought, and it flowers at its tips.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Plague.** A drifting green mist of germs.
Sinks slowly and fades. Any animal it touches falls sick: it runs a fever, passes the sickness to animals it touches, and dies of it unless medicine reaches it. Heat kills the germs.
*Try:* Put a group of mice or ants on the floor and release a puff of plague near them: they fall sick one after another. Sprinkle medicine to save them.

**Medicine.** Crushed pink tablets, a light powder.
Falls and piles. A sick animal it touches is cured, its fever falls, and it stops spreading the sickness.
*Try:* Sprinkle it over sick animals (after plague): they get better and stop spreading it.

**Mould.** A fuzzy grey-green mould.
Stays put and spreads slowly over damp food (meat, eggs, flour paste, honey) wherever there is water beside it, turning it into more mould. Dry, it stops; heat kills it.
*Try:* Put a piece of meat next to a little water, and a dot of mould on the meat: grey-green mould spreads over the meat.

**Spores.** Fine brown fungus spores, a very light powder.
Drifts down and settles. On damp soil, out of the light, they sprout into mushrooms. A cloud of the dust flashes into flame from a spark.
*Try:* Build a roof over some damp dirt, then sprinkle spores under it: mushrooms grow in the dark. In daylight nothing grows.

**Mushroom.** A pale living mushroom.
Grows a short stalk from damp soil, but only in the dark: light stops it. It drinks from the soil, withers when dry, and snails and mice eat it.
*Try:* Grow it from spores on damp dirt under a roof. Snails and mice eat it.

**Algae.** Green pond scum, a light living powder.
Floats on water and spreads through it while daylight reaches it, giving off bubbles of oxygen. Fish eat it.
*Try:* Pour a pool of water on the floor, sprinkle a little algae on top, and leave it in open daylight: in about half a minute it spreads green across the water and bubbles out oxygen. Fish eat it.

**Moss.** Soft green moss.
Stays put, clinging to stone, brick, concrete and wood, and creeps over them where it is damp and shaded. Dry heat kills it.
*Try:* Build a stone wall under a roof with a pool of water near its foot, and put a dot of moss on the wall: it slowly creeps over the damp stone.

**Glowing plankton.** Sea water full of tiny glowing plankton.
Flows like water. Calm, it is dark; stirred, poured or splashed, it flashes bright blue where it moves.
*Try:* Pour a pool of it on the floor, then pour sand into it or sweep the Wind tool across it: it flashes blue where it moves, then goes dark.

**Caterpillar.** A plump green caterpillar.
Crawls over the ground and plants, munching leaves, grass and plants. After a few good meals it spins itself into a cocoon.
*Try:* Grow some grass or a tree on damp dirt, then put a few caterpillars on it: they eat leaves and, after a few meals, turn into cocoons that hatch butterflies.

**Cocoon.** A silk cocoon with a caterpillar changing inside.
Stays put; after a while a butterfly breaks out. It burns easily.
*Try:* Make a heap and touch it with fire: it catches and burns.

**Butterfly.** A bright butterfly.
Flutters through the open air, turning flowers into seeds as it feeds on them, and lays its eggs on leaves, where they hatch as caterpillars. Water drowns it; heat or cold kill it.
*Try:* Grow a garden with flowers and a tree (seeds and an acorn on damp dirt, daylight, a thin rain cloud above), then release butterflies nearby: they fly to the flowers and leaves, making seeds and caterpillars.
